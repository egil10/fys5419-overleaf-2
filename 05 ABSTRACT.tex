\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Picking a small basket of equities to balance expected return against risk is the oldest problem in quantitative finance. The continuous version is convex and has a closed form, but the moment a fund insists on holding exactly $K$ of $n$ candidate stocks the problem becomes combinatorial and, in principle, hard. That tension has made constrained portfolio selection a flagship test case for near-term quantum optimisation, and a natural question for a course on quantum computing is whether the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA) actually helps.

This report answers the question on a single, sharply specified instance. We pick sixteen equities from four contrasting baskets (mega-cap tech, quantum-computing pure-plays, large-cap quantum-adjacent firms, and a macro-opposed mix of an oil major, an airline, a gold miner, and a bank), estimate returns and covariance from three years of daily prices, and ask QAOA to choose four of them. The pipeline is built from scratch in pure NumPy, without Qiskit or PennyLane, so every algorithmic choice is exposed and auditable. Eight experiments are run on the same canonical instance: a single benchmark run, the $p=1$ energy landscape, four parameter sweeps (depth, problem size, risk aversion, budget penalty), a warm-start ablation that tests whether non-monotone depth scaling is structural, and a head-to-head with a constraint-preserving XY-ring mixer paired with a Dicke initial state.

The empirical picture is consistent and sobering. Five classical baselines (brute force, greedy by single-asset Sharpe, rounded continuous Markowitz, simulated annealing, and an exact mixed-integer programme) all return the same four-asset basket of GOOGL, IBM, NVDA, and JPM in milliseconds. Standard penalty-mixer QAOA at depth three reaches an apparently respectable scaled approximation ratio of about $0.99$, but its highest-probability outcome is the infeasible all-ones state and the four next most likely outcomes are infeasible single-asset selections. The algorithm has learnt the broad shape of the penalty term and not the cardinality constraint it was meant to enforce. Two well-known fixes close most of the residual gap: warm-started angles restore monotone scaling of the approximation ratio with circuit depth, and the XY-Dicke pipeline drives the probability of a feasible outcome to one by construction at every depth tested, lifting the approximation ratio to $0.9995$. No quantum advantage is claimed; the contribution is methodological, a transparent characterisation of where standard penalty QAOA fails on cardinality-constrained mean--variance portfolios and a clean empirical confirmation of the two structural fixes the literature has proposed.
\end{abstract}
