\section{Methods}\label{section:methods}

\subsection{Portfolio Optimization and Combinatorial Constraints}

The portfolio optimization problem seeks to allocate capital across $n$ assets such that
risk is minimized subject to a return target. In the Markowitz mean–variance framework
\parencite{markowitz1952}, the continuous relaxation is
\[
  \min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^n} \; w^T \Sigma w - \mu^T w,
\]
where $w$ is the weight vector, $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the asset
covariance matrix, and $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the vector of expected returns.
Classical solutions scale polynomially in $n$, but adding a \textit{cardinality
constraint}—restricting the portfolio to exactly $K$ out of $N$ assets—renders the
problem NP-hard \parencite{hastie}. This combinatorial structure motivates a quantum
approach.

\subsection{Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO)}

To apply QAOA, the portfolio selection problem is reformulated as a Quadratic
Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO). Introducing binary decision variables
$x_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicating whether asset $i$ is included, the objective becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qubo}
  \min_{x \in \{0,1\}^n}
    \left(
      q\,x^T\Sigma x
      - \mu^T x
      + \lambda\,(\mathbf{1}^T x - B)^2
    \right),
\end{equation}
where $q > 0$ is a risk-tolerance parameter, $B$ is the target number of assets
(the budget constraint), and $\lambda \gg 1$ is a penalty coefficient that
forces the optimizer to respect the cardinality condition. The quadratic penalty
term in \cref{eq:qubo} vanishes when exactly $B$ assets are selected, reducing
the problem to the pure risk–return trade-off.

Daily log-returns are used throughout to ensure stationarity and comparability
across price levels:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logret}
  r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right).
\end{equation}

\subsection{QAOA Circuit Architecture}

The QAOA ansatz \parencite{zhou2018qaoa} alternates between a \textit{cost unitary}
$U_C(\gamma)$ that encodes the QUBO objective and a \textit{mixer unitary}
$U_B(\beta)$ that explores the binary solution space. For $p$ alternating layers,
the parameterized state is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qaoa_state}
  |\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle
  = U_B(\beta_p)\,U_C(\gamma_p)\cdots U_B(\beta_1)\,U_C(\gamma_1)\,|+\rangle^{\otimes n}.
\end{equation}
The initial state $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$ is prepared by applying a Hadamard gate
to each of the $n$ qubits. The cost unitary applies $ZZ$-interaction phase rotations
proportional to the QUBO energy, while the standard $X$-mixer applies $R_X(2\beta)$
rotations to each qubit. The variational parameters
$(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}) \in \mathbb{R}^{2p}$ are optimized
classically to minimize the expected cost
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
  F_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
  = \langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|C|\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the diagonal cost operator corresponding to \cref{eq:qubo}.
Increasing $p$ allows the circuit to explore more of the solution landscape,
with the exact solution recovered in the limit $p \to \infty$
\parencite{giovagnoli_qaoa_intro}.

\subsection{Classical--Quantum Hybrid Optimization Loop}

Optimization proceeds via a classical outer loop that iteratively updates
$(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})$ based on the expectation value returned
by quantum circuit evaluations. The gradient-free COBYLA optimizer is used for
low-$p$ runs; L-BFGS-B with parameter-shift gradients is used for deeper circuits.
Circuit execution is performed using Qiskit's statevector simulator for noiseless
benchmarking and the \texttt{qasm\_simulator} for shot-noise experiments
\parencite{ibm_qaoa_tutorial}. The approximation ratio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:approx_ratio}
  r = \frac{F_p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*,\boldsymbol{\beta}^*)}{F_{\mathrm{opt}}}
\end{equation}
measures solution quality relative to the exact brute-force optimum $F_{\mathrm{opt}}$,
which is tractable for small asset universes ($n \lesssim 20$).

\subsection{Dataset and Regime Selection}

Assets are drawn from three classes representing distinct risk factors: equities
(S\&P 500 ETF, SPY), fixed income (US 10-year Treasury ETF, TLT), and energy
(energy sector ETF, XLE). The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ and return vector $\mu$
are estimated from daily log-returns during three historical crisis windows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Global Financial Crisis} — September 2008 to March 2009 ($\approx 150$ trading days);
  \item \textbf{COVID-19 crash} — February to April 2020 ($\approx 50$ trading days);
  \item \textbf{2022 inflation shock} — January to December 2022 ($\approx 250$ trading days).
\end{itemize}
Focusing on crisis regimes removes the low-information ``quiet'' periods that
dominate full-history estimation and ensures that $\Sigma$ reflects elevated
volatility and correlation breakdowns characteristic of stress conditions—the
most demanding test for a portfolio optimizer.
