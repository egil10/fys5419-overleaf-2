\section{Introduction}\label{section:Introduction}

Modern portfolio theory begins with the mean--variance framework of \textcite{markowitz1952}, in which an investor trades expected return against the variance of a weighted basket of assets. In its continuous form the problem is a convex quadratic programme with a closed-form solution, but practical mandates almost always overlay discrete structure: a fund may have to hold exactly $K$ of $n$ candidate equities, allocate in integer lots, or respect sector caps. Imposing the cardinality constraint $\sum_i x_i = K$ on binary selectors $\boldsymbol{x} \in \{0,1\}^n$ collapses the convex programme onto a combinatorial lattice of size $\binom{n}{K}$ and turns mean--variance optimisation into a binary quadratic programme that is NP-hard in general. Brute-force enumeration over $\boldsymbol{z} \in \{0,1\}^n$ scales as $2^n$. At the $n=16$ instance studied here the search space is $2^{16} \approx 65{,}536$ bitstrings, enumerable in 30 milliseconds on a laptop yet already large enough to expose the structure quantum optimisation tries to exploit. The same Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation (QUBO) form recurs across MAX-CUT, the travelling salesman problem, and discrete knapsack-style allocation \parencite{knapsack2024qaoa}, so the question of how to attack such cost landscapes reaches well beyond finance.

The Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA), introduced by \textcite{farhi2014qaoa}, recasts QUBO minimisation as a ground-state preparation problem for an Ising Hamiltonian $H_C$. A variational ansatz alternates $p$ layers of a cost unitary $e^{-i\gamma_k H_C}$ with a mixing unitary $e^{-i\beta_k H_M}$ acting on an initial uniform superposition, and the $2p$ angles $(\boldsymbol\gamma, \boldsymbol\beta)$ are tuned by a classical outer loop. The algorithm is most naturally motivated as a Trotterised, variational analogue of quantum adiabatic evolution: in the limit $p \to \infty$ with an appropriate schedule, the ansatz recovers the exact adiabatic algorithm and converges to the true ground state \parencite{zhou2018qaoa}. The mechanism is constructive interference: the cost unitary writes a problem-dependent phase, and the mixer turns that phase pattern into probability concentrated on low-energy strings. With $n$ qubits, depth $O(p)$, and only $2p$ variational parameters, QAOA can in principle run on present-day hardware without fault tolerance, making it a flagship Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) algorithm and a close cousin of the variational quantum eigensolver \parencite{tilly2022vqe}; a substantial pedagogical literature has grown up around its mechanism \parencite{giovagnoli_qaoa_intro}.

Portfolio selection has become a flagship QAOA use case, and the past two years have produced a focused literature. Representative work includes a depth-and-noise benchmark across realistic asset universes \parencite{stopfer2025benchmark}, a constraint-preserving XY-mixer that keeps the state inside the cardinality-feasible subspace \parencite{mancilla2026constrained}, a slack-variable encoding for inequality-constrained portfolio QUBOs \parencite{thomassin2025slack}, a noisy-device study of the gap between idealised and realised performance \parencite{shen2025noisy}, a head-to-head comparison against quantum annealing \parencite{morapakula2025annealing}, an operator-pool strategy for reducing measurement overhead \parencite{soloviev2025pauli}, and circuit-cutting techniques aimed at pushing simulators beyond the $n=16$ regime \parencite{soloviev2025cutting}. The conclusion is consistent: QAOA probes QUBO landscapes informatively but does not yet rival classical solvers at practical scale.

Honesty about what QAOA does and does not deliver is essential. No quantum advantage has been demonstrated for QAOA on any useful problem, portfolio selection included, and classical baselines comfortably outperform QAOA at the depths achievable on current devices. The optimisation step is plagued by barren plateaus, in which the gradient of $E(\boldsymbol\gamma, \boldsymbol\beta)$ vanishes exponentially in $n$ for random initialisations of sufficiently deep circuits, and the depth $p$ required to compete with classical heuristics on hard instances appears to grow with system size, eroding the constant-depth NISQ picture. At our $n=16$ instance, brute force returns the certified optimum in roughly 30 ms; a single QAOA run with $p=3$ and ten COBYLA restarts takes about 132 s, four thousand times slower for a worse answer. We therefore treat QAOA as a research vehicle for studying cost-landscape geometry, depth scaling, and the interference mechanism, rather than a tool we expect to beat Gurobi on a 16-equity Markowitz instance.

The work reported here is methodological and is built around a single, sharp empirical finding. We implement QAOA from scratch in pure NumPy, constructing the statevector simulator, the Ising-mapped cost Hamiltonian, and the COBYLA-driven outer loop without recourse to high-level variational libraries such as Qiskit or PennyLane. The pipeline is derived end to end: daily equity prices over 2023-01-03 to 2025-12-30 (751 observations on a 16-asset universe drawn from the mag7, quantum, quantum-big, and anti baskets) yield moments $(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$, folded into a penalised QUBO with cardinality term $A(\sum_i x_i - K)^2$, mapped to an Ising Hamiltonian, and realised as a Pauli-$Z$ cost operator $H_C$ paired with a transverse-field mixer $H_M$. Every run is validated against brute-force enumeration. The instance turns out to be a falsifying test: three independent classical heuristics --- exhaustive enumeration, greedy-Sharpe selection, and a rounded continuous Markowitz solution, with simulated annealing as a fourth check --- all converge in milliseconds to the same basket, $\{\text{GOOGL}, \text{IBM}, \text{NVDA}, \text{JPM}\}$. QAOA at $p=3$ reaches a scaled approximation ratio $r \approx 0.94$, which sounds respectable, yet its top-1 most probable bitstring is the all-ones state ``pick every asset'' and ranks two through five are single-asset quantum stocks. The algorithm gets confused about what ``pick four'' means and votes instead for ``pick sixteen'' followed by ``pick one''; probability on the true optimum sits near $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$. Five experiments anatomise this failure: a canonical single-run benchmark locates QAOA on the cost spectrum; a full $p=1$ energy landscape over $(\gamma, \beta)$ serves as a diagnostic visualising the basins and saddles the outer loop must navigate; and four sweeps vary one knob at a time --- depth $p$, problem size $n$, risk aversion $\lambda$, and budget penalty $A$ --- to characterise when and why the interference mechanism breaks down on cardinality-constrained portfolios.
