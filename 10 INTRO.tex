\section{Introduction}\label{section:Introduction}

Modern portfolio theory begins with the mean--variance framework of \textcite{markowitz1952}, in which an investor trades expected return against the variance of a weighted basket of assets. In its original continuous form the problem is a convex quadratic programme with a closed-form solution. Practical mandates, however, almost always impose discrete structure: a fund may be required to hold exactly $K$ out of $n$ candidate equities, to allocate in integer lot sizes, or to respect sector caps. Imposing a cardinality constraint $\sum_i x_i = K$ on binary selectors $\boldsymbol{x} \in \{0,1\}^n$ turns the convex programme into a binary quadratic programme, and the feasible set collapses from a continuous simplex to a combinatorial lattice of size $\binom{n}{K}$. The resulting Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation (QUBO) is NP-hard in general, and brute-force enumeration scales as $2^n$ --- tractable at the $n=16$ instance studied here but intractable at the hundreds of assets characterising institutional universes. The same QUBO form recurs across combinatorial optimisation, encompassing MAX-CUT, the travelling salesman problem, graph colouring, and discrete knapsack-style allocation problems \parencite{knapsack2024qaoa}, so the algorithmic question of how to attack such cost landscapes has implications well beyond finance.

The Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA), introduced by \textcite{farhi2014qaoa}, recasts QUBO minimisation as a ground-state preparation problem for an Ising Hamiltonian $H_C$. A variational ansatz alternates $p$ layers of a cost unitary $e^{-i\gamma_k H_C}$ with a mixing unitary $e^{-i\beta_k H_M}$ acting on an initial uniform superposition, and the $2p$ angles $(\boldsymbol\gamma, \boldsymbol\beta)$ are tuned by a classical outer loop. The algorithm is most naturally motivated as a Trotterised, variational analogue of quantum adiabatic evolution: in the limit $p \to \infty$ with an appropriate schedule, the ansatz recovers the exact adiabatic algorithm and converges to the true ground state \parencite{zhou2018qaoa}. Three properties make QAOA a flagship Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) algorithm. The circuit has only $n$ qubits, depth $O(p)$, and $2p$ variational parameters, so unlike Shor's algorithm or HHL it can in principle run on present-day hardware without fault tolerance. The hybrid quantum--classical structure also makes QAOA a close cousin of the variational quantum eigensolver \parencite{tilly2022vqe}, and a substantial pedagogical literature has grown up around its mechanism \parencite{giovagnoli_qaoa_intro}. The portfolio-selection use case in particular has attracted recent attention, ranging from systematic benchmarks of QAOA on portfolio QUBOs \parencite{stopfer2025benchmark}, constraint-preserving mixer designs \parencite{mancilla2026constrained}, and slack-variable formulations \parencite{thomassin2025slack}, to noisy-device simulations \parencite{shen2025noisy}, comparisons against quantum annealing \parencite{morapakula2025annealing}, and an operator-pool strategy aimed at reducing measurement overhead \parencite{soloviev2025pauli}, and circuit-cutting techniques designed to push the simulator beyond the $n=16$ regime \parencite{soloviev2025cutting}.

Honesty about what QAOA does and does not deliver is essential. No quantum advantage has yet been demonstrated for QAOA on any useful problem; classical baselines such as Goemans--Williamson for MAX-CUT and modern mixed-integer solvers for portfolio selection comfortably outperform QAOA at the depths achievable on current devices. The classical optimisation step is plagued by barren plateaus, in which the gradient of the energy expectation $E(\boldsymbol\gamma, \boldsymbol\beta)$ vanishes exponentially in $n$ for random initialisations of sufficiently deep circuits. Worse, the depth $p$ required to compete with classical heuristics on hard instances appears to grow with system size, eroding the NISQ-friendly constant-depth picture in practice. We therefore treat QAOA in this report as a research vehicle for studying cost-landscape geometry and depth scaling, not as a tool we expect to beat classical solvers on a 16-equity Markowitz instance.

The work reported here is methodological. We implement QAOA from scratch in pure NumPy, building the statevector simulator, the Ising-mapped cost Hamiltonian, and the COBYLA-driven outer loop without recourse to high-level variational libraries such as Qiskit or PennyLane. The pipeline is derived end-to-end: financial price series $\rightarrow$ first and second moments $(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma) \rightarrow$ penalised QUBO with cardinality term $A(\sum_i x_i - K)^2 \rightarrow$ Ising Hamiltonian $\rightarrow$ Pauli-$Z$ cost operator $H_C$ with transverse-field mixer $H_M$ $\rightarrow$ QAOA circuit. Every run on the 16-equity instance is validated against the brute-force optimum (roughly 30 ms by direct enumeration), so we always know how well QAOA is performing in absolute terms. Around that core implementation we conduct four parameter sweeps: circuit depth $p$, problem size $n$, the risk-aversion coefficient $\lambda$, and the budget-penalty strength $A$. The depth sweep tracks the approximation ratio $r(p)$; the size sweep probes how favourable angles transfer as $n$ grows; the $\lambda$ sweep walks the efficient frontier; and the $A$ sweep characterises the trade-off between feasibility enforcement and conditioning of the cost landscape. The question we ask is mechanistic --- when, and to what extent, do quantum interference and the adiabatic intuition help on this family of instances --- rather than whether QAOA wins a wall-clock race against a classical solver, which at $n = 16$ it does not.

The remainder of the report is organised as follows. \Cref{section:methods} develops the formalism from the Markowitz objective through the QUBO and Ising mappings to the full QAOA circuit, including a constraint-preserving XY-mixer variant. \Cref{section:impl} describes the pure-NumPy implementation, the equity dataset, and the experimental protocol. \Cref{section:result} reports the four parameter sweeps together with cost-landscape visualisations and brute-force comparisons. \Cref{section:conclusion} summarises the findings and revisits the honest caveats raised above.
