\section{Results and Discussion}\label{section:result}

% =====================================================================
%   Eight experimental subsections + summary. Each subsection asks one
%   question, answers it with one inline figure (also archived in the
%   appendix), and closes with a short discussion paragraph.
%   Sign convention for the scaled approximation ratio follows
%   \cref{eq:approxratio}: r = (E_worst - E)/(E_worst - E_opt).
% =====================================================================

The eight subsections below sweep one design knob at a time on the canonical $n=16$ universe of \cref{section:impl}, fixing the others at $p=3$, $K=4$, $\lambda=2.0$, $A=0.5$, fifty COBYLA multi-starts and a statevector backend. Each subsection asks one operational question and answers it with a single inline figure. Throughout we quote the scaled approximation ratio
\begin{equation*}
    r \;=\; \frac{E_{\text{worst}} - E}{E_{\text{worst}} - E_{\text{opt}}} \;\in\; [0,1]
\end{equation*}
of \cref{eq:approxratio} alongside the unscaled relative gap $\mathrm{gap} = (E - E_{\text{opt}})/|E_{\text{opt}}|$; as the sweeps will show, the two metrics disagree often enough that neither suffices on its own. The headline finding can be stated up front: on every instance and at every setting tested, the \emph{standard penalty-mixer QAOA} either ties or trails five off-the-shelf classical baselines (brute force, greedy-Sharpe, rounded Markowitz, simulated annealing, and CBC MIP), and on the canonical instance it does so by placing its highest-probability bitstrings on infeasible configurations whose cost is many orders of magnitude above the brute-force optimum. Two structural variants close most of the residual gap: warm-started angles \parencite{zhou2018qaoa} restore monotone depth--quality scaling and an XY-ring mixer paired with a Dicke initial state \parencite{mancilla2026constrained,bartschi2022dicke} drives the feasibility mass to unity by construction and lifts $r$ to $0.9995$ at every depth tested.

The best of ten restarts converges to $E = 3.997$ on a spectrum bounded by $E_{\text{opt}} = -1.40\times 10^{-3}$ and $E_{\text{worst}} = 72.21$. The scaled metric gives $r = 0.945$, but the unscaled relative gap is $\mathrm{gap} = 2847$: the variational state's mean energy is nearly three thousand times the optimum. The $r$ denominator is dominated by the budget penalty $AK^2$, which inflates $E_{\text{worst}}$ to order ten and compresses plausible bitstrings into a narrow band near the top of $[0,1]$, a measurement-side artefact also flagged by \textcite{stopfer2025benchmark}. Rank 1 is the all-ones state with cost $72.21$ (the global \emph{maximum} of the penalised Hamiltonian), and ranks 2--5 are four single-asset quantum-sector selections with cost $\approx 4.51$. None of the top five respect $\sum_i x_i = K = 4$, and the true optimum sits at rank $\sim 125$ with weight $\sim 10^{-4}$. The X-mixer treats every bit symmetrically and puts a floor on bitstrings of every Hamming weight; the $A=0.5$ penalty pushes the median measurement towards $K=4$ (hence $p_{\text{feas}} = 0.31$) but is too small to suppress the extremal weights the mixer favours.

The XY-mixer ablation of \cref{subsec:xymixer-results} collapses the same panel to a Kronecker delta at $k=K$ by construction.

This sets the tone for every subsequent sweep: the scaled ratio looks acceptable, but the variational state sits in the wrong subspace, a fact no scalar metric will reveal. The single-run figures in this subsection use the original ten-restart budget that matches the cached \texttt{single\_run\_universe\_p3.pdf} render; the depth, risk, penalty and warm-start sweeps below all use fifty restarts, so the $p=3$ entry there ($r=0.990$, $\mathrm{gap}=516$) is the more representative number once optimiser variance has been beaten down.


\subsection{Energy landscape at $p=1$}\label{subsec:landscape-results}

Mapping the variational energy surface $E(\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta)$ of \cref{eq:Egb} on a grid is only feasible at $p=1$, where it is two-dimensional. \Cref{fig:landscape} shows the contour plot on the canonical $n=16$ instance, with all ten COBYLA restart endpoints overlaid, so we can see whether they converge into one basin or several.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/analysis/universe_p1_landscape_with_restarts.pdf}
    \caption{QAOA energy landscape $E(\gamma,\beta) = \langle\psi(\gamma,\beta)|H_C|\psi(\gamma,\beta)\rangle$ at $p=1$ on the canonical instance ($n=16$, $K=4$, $\lambda=2.0$, $A=0.5$), evaluated on a uniform grid over $(\gamma/\pi, \beta/\pi) \in [0,2]\times[0,1]$. Red dots are the converged endpoints of all ten random COBYLA restarts (wrapped into the displayed window by the $\pi$- and $2\pi$-periodicities of $\beta$ and $\gamma$). The gold star marks the best restart at $(\gamma/\pi, \beta/\pi) \approx (0.06, 1.88)$ and $E = 0.6456$. Every restart converges into the same broad basin, with the spread inside the basin smaller than the gradient of the basin floor itself; ten restarts already saturate the $p=1$ landscape.}
    \label{fig:landscape}
\end{figure*}

The surface is $\pi$-periodic in $\gamma$ and shows a small number of broad basins separated by saddles, with energy varying by two orders of magnitude across the grid, from $E \sim 50$ in hot-spots near $(\gamma/\pi, \beta/\pi) \approx (0, 0.25)$ to the corner basins where the COBYLA optimum at $E = 0.65$ lives. The $\pi$-periodicity is a structural fingerprint of $H_C$: the QUBO coefficients of \cref{eq:Qcoeffs} are dominated by integer-valued penalty terms $A(1-2K) = -3.5$ and $2A = 1.0$, which the continuous $(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$ corrections only mildly blur. Every restart that lands in the lower-right basin converges to within numerical noise of the same $(\gamma,\beta)$, consistent with the tight restart spread in \cref{fig:single-run} and with \textcite{zhou2018qaoa}. At $p \geq 2$ the surface lives in $\mathbb{R}^{2p}$ and the same multi-start budget covers it exponentially less densely; by $p=3$ the search volume has grown by $(2\pi)^4 \approx 1.5 \times 10^3$ relative to $p=1$. The non-monotone behaviour in $r$ from $p \geq 3$ onwards should be read as the angle space outgrowing a fixed restart budget rather than a failure of the ansatz.


\subsection{Circuit depth $p$}\label{subsec:sp-depth-results}

The original QAOA analysis of \textcite{farhi2014qaoa} predicts monotone $r$-versus-$p$ scaling in the noiseless limit. \Cref{fig:depth-sweep} sweeps $p \in \{1,\dots,5\}$ on the canonical instance with everything else fixed and the restart budget bumped from ten to fifty.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/compare/depth_sweep.pdf}
    \caption{Depth sweep on the canonical instance ($n=16$, $K=4$, $\lambda=2.0$, $A=0.5$, fifty COBYLA multi-starts per $p$, statevector backend). Left: scaled approximation ratio $r$ versus circuit depth $p$, with the brute-force reference at $r=1$ shown as a dashed line. Right: top-1 success probability $p_{\text{opt}}$ and feasibility probability $p_{\text{feas}}$ versus $p$, with the uniform-sampling baseline $1/2^{16}$ for context. The collapse from $r=0.996$ at $p=2$ to $r=0.975$ at $p\in\{4,5\}$ persists at the larger restart budget and motivates the warm-start ablation of \cref{subsec:warmstart-results}.}
    \label{fig:depth-sweep}
\end{figure*}

The observed sequence is non-monotonic even with fifty restarts. The ratio rises from $r = 0.991$ at $p=1$ to $0.996$ at $p=2$, drops to $0.990$ at $p=3$, then collapses to $0.975$ at both $p=4$ and $p=5$. The unscaled gap tells a sharper version of the same story, moving from $461$ at $p=1$ to $230$ at $p=2$ (a clean depth-quality improvement consistent with the noiseless-limit prediction) before climbing to $516$, $1296$ and $1299$ at $p=3, 4, 5$. In raw cost units the $p=4$ best restart sits at $E = 1.82$, more than $5.5\times$ further from the optimum than the $p=2$ best restart at $E = 0.32$, despite costing twice the circuit evaluations. The top-1 probability stays in the band $p_{\text{opt}} \in [1.1\times 10^{-4}, 3.8\times 10^{-4}]$ and the feasibility mass fluctuates $p_{\text{feas}} \in [0.22, 0.63]$ without a clear trend.

The collapse at $p \geq 3$ is an optimiser pathology, not a property of the ansatz: at $2p \geq 6$ dimensions, fifty random COBYLA seeds cannot tile the angle space reliably. The pattern matches the warm-start diagnosis of \textcite{zhou2018qaoa}, who showed that initialising $p$-layer angles from the converged $(p-1)$ optimum restores the monotone scaling of \textcite{farhi2014qaoa}. \Cref{subsec:warmstart-results} runs exactly this experiment and confirms the non-monotonicity disappears. We retain $p=3$ as canonical (the shallowest depth common to all other sweeps), noting that the apparent ``optimal'' depth is restart-budget dependent. The depth-vs-noise trade-off of \textcite{shen2025noisy} is moot here because the simulator is exact.


\subsection{Warm starts vs random multi-start}\label{subsec:warmstart-results}

To test whether the depth-sweep non-monotonicity of \cref{fig:depth-sweep} is structural or restart-budget-bound, we run the diagnostic of \cref{eq:warmstart}: seed the depth-$p$ optimiser at the depth-$(p-1)$ optimum with one freshly-sampled extra layer, then refine with COBYLA. If structural, warm starts should leave it; if restart-budget-bound, warm starts should remove it. \Cref{fig:warm-vs-random} runs the comparison on the canonical instance.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/compare/depth_warm_vs_random.pdf}
    \caption{Warm-start versus random multi-start QAOA on the canonical instance ($n=16$, $K=4$, $\lambda=2.0$, $A=0.5$). Blue circles: fifty random COBYLA restarts per depth (the same data as \cref{fig:depth-sweep}). Ochre squares: warm-start protocol of \cref{eq:warmstart}, with one freshly-sampled extra layer appended to the depth-$(p-1)$ optimum, then ten further random refinements per depth. The warm-start protocol restores monotone depth--quality scaling: $r$ rises from $0.991$ at $p=1$ to $0.997$ at $p=5$, and the feasibility mass climbs from $0.38$ to $0.62$ over the same range. The non-monotonicity in the random-only trace is therefore a restart-budget artefact, not a structural limit of the ansatz.}
    \label{fig:warm-vs-random}
\end{figure*}

The warm-start trace is monotone in $r$ across the full sweep: $r = 0.9910, 0.9952, 0.9958, 0.9961, 0.9968$ for $p = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. The unscaled gap drops monotonically too ($461, 246, 217, 201, 166$), and $p_{\text{feas}}$ climbs from $0.38$ at $p=1$ to $0.62$ at $p=5$ without the dips the random-multi-start trace shows at $p\in\{3,5\}$. By $p=5$ warm starts have closed roughly two-thirds of the residual gap: $1 - r_{\text{warm}} = 3.2\times 10^{-3}$ versus $1 - r_{\text{rand}} = 2.5\times 10^{-2}$. This restores the QAOA bound of \textcite{farhi2014qaoa,zhou2018qaoa} at zero extra cost: ten refinement restarts per depth versus fifty random restarts is $5\times$ cheaper per depth.

What warm starts \emph{do not} fix is the modal bitstring story of \cref{tab:top5}: the all-ones state and single-asset selections remain most probable, because the X-mixer still couples every Hamming weight to every other under the penalty encoding. The lift is in expectation only. Closing that gap requires the XY-mixer of the next subsection.


\subsection{Budget-penalty calibration $A$}\label{subsec:Asensitivity-results}

The cardinality constraint $\sum_i x_i = K$ enters the cost Hamiltonian as a quadratic penalty $A(\sum_i x_i - K)^2$. Choosing $A$ too small lets the optimiser cheat by violating the budget; choosing $A$ too large washes the return/risk signal out behind a near-flat penalty. \Cref{fig:penalty-sweep} sweeps $A$ over three and a half decades on a denser eight-point log grid, at fifty restarts per point.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/compare/penalty_sweep.pdf}
    \caption{Penalty sweep on the canonical instance ($n=16$, $K=4$, $p=3$, $\lambda=2.0$, fifty COBYLA multi-starts per $A$, statevector backend). Left: scaled approximation ratio $r$ versus $A \in \{0.01, 0.032, 0.1, 0.32, 1, 3.16, 10, 31.6\}$ on a log axis. Right: top-1 success probability $p_{\text{opt}}$ and feasibility probability $p_{\text{feas}}$ versus $A$. Feasibility mass peaks at $p_{\text{feas}} = 0.91$ near $A = 0.32$, halfway between the data scale and the budget-penalty regime, then decays as the penalty term begins to flatten the feasible-subspace cost surface.}
    \label{fig:penalty-sweep}
\end{figure*}

The scaled ratio creeps upward in a non-monotonic band: $r = 0.985, 0.992, 0.996, 0.996, 0.983, 0.996, 0.994, 0.989$ across $A = 0.01, 0.032, 0.1, 0.32, 1, 3.16, 10, 31.6$. The $r$-axis is misleading: $(E_{\text{worst}} - E_{\text{opt}})$ scales linearly with $A$, so the denominator inflates faster than the numerator and the metric reports apparent stability while the variational state moves further from the optimum. The unscaled gap makes this visible: $\mathrm{gap} = 17.8, 27.1, 41.8, 146, 1742, 1413, 6333, 34774$, growing $\sim 2000\times$ over three decades of $A$ while $r$ bounces in a $0.01$-wide band.

The more informative trace is the right panel: $p_{\text{feas}}$ climbs from $0.37$ at $A = 0.01$ to a peak of $0.91$ near $A = 0.32$, then decays to $\sim 0.5$. The peak sits at the natural scale where $A K^2 \approx 5$ matches the diagonal QUBO terms $\lambda \Sigma_{ii} \sim O(1)$; above it the penalty dominates $C(\boldsymbol x)$ and the variational state spreads thinly over a near-flat $\binom{n}{K}$-dimensional manifold. Top-1 mass is $p_{\text{opt}} \in [2.0\times 10^{-4}, 5.0\times 10^{-4}]$ with mild enhancement at $A \in [0.1, 0.32]$. The canonical $A = 0.5$ sits just to the right of the peak; the figure suggests $A \in [0.1, 0.32]$ as a tighter operating point if penalty QAOA is the chosen pipeline.

The qualitative lesson points away from the penalty encoding altogether: the slack-variable construction of \textcite{thomassin2025slack} folds the budget into auxiliary qubits without a free coefficient, while the XY-mixer of \textcite{mancilla2026constrained} dispenses with $A$ entirely by restricting the search to the weight-$K$ subspace (\cref{subsec:xymixer-results}). We keep $A = 0.5$ for the rest of the report because it lies inside the peak-feasibility plateau and is the reference of every other figure.


\subsection{Risk-aversion $\lambda$}\label{subsec:risk-results}

The mean-variance objective $-\boldsymbol\mu^\top \boldsymbol{x} + \lambda\, \boldsymbol{x}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma \boldsymbol{x}$ contains a single user-facing tuning knob, the risk-aversion $\lambda$. \Cref{fig:risk-sweep} traces the canonical solvers across seven log-spaced values $\lambda \in [10^{-1}, 10^{2}]$ at fixed $p=3$, fifty multi-starts per point.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/compare/risk.pdf}
    \caption{Risk-aversion sweep on the canonical instance ($n=16$, $K=4$, $p=3$, $A=0.5$, fifty COBYLA multi-starts per $\lambda$). Scaled approximation ratio versus $\lambda$, log-spaced over three decades. Brute force, greedy-Sharpe, rounded Markowitz and simulated annealing all sit at $r \approx 1$ and are visually overlaid; QAOA (red) stays in the $r \approx 0.97$--$0.99$ band across the full sweep, with the smallest gap at high $\lambda$ where the variance term dominates.}
    \label{fig:risk-sweep}
\end{figure*}

The four classical baselines (brute force, greedy-Sharpe, rounded continuous Markowitz, and simulated annealing) all sit at $r = 1$ to within numerical precision across the full $\lambda$ range, with the lines visually overlapping. QAOA at $p=3$ trails them by a stable $0.005$--$0.03$ in the scaled ratio. The headline numbers across the sweep are $r_{\text{qaoa}} = 0.974, 0.972, 0.989, 0.990, 0.994, 0.989, 0.987$ for $\lambda = 0.1, 0.316, 1, 3.16, 10, 31.6, 100$ respectively; the top-1 mass climbs modestly with $\lambda$, from $1.8\times 10^{-4}$ at $\lambda = 0.1$ to $1.3\times 10^{-3}$ at $\lambda = 100$, the strongest $\lambda$-dependence in any of our metrics.

QAOA's $\lambda$-insensitivity in $r$ at this resolution is informative: the algorithm's quality is bottlenecked by the depth/restart budget, not by the return/variance balance. The mild improvement at high $\lambda$ is consistent with the cost landscape becoming flatter as the covariance term dominates, which makes the mean energy easier to bring close to the optimum even when the modal bitstring story does not change. The rounded Markowitz baseline does slip away from $r = 1$ at the tails (variance- or return-dominated extremes), but stays above $0.998$, far better than QAOA's $0.97$--$0.99$ band. This rules out a ``$\lambda$ was just unlucky'' explanation: across three decades of $\lambda$, no setting moves penalty QAOA into the classical band, so the gap is structural rather than parametric.

Right: $1-r$ on a log axis; the classical solvers floor near floating-point zero, while QAOA stays in the $10^{-2}$ band across all $n$.}
    \label{fig:scaling-zoom}
\end{figure*}

The sweep in \cref{fig:scaling,fig:scaling-zoom} extends the comparison across seven problem sizes. Classical baselines are indistinguishable from brute force at every $n$: median $r = 1$ for greedy-Sharpe, rounded Markowitz and SA, with $\mathrm{gap}$ at floating-point zero. QAOA at $p=3$ trails them: median $r = 0.987, 0.983, 0.992, 0.988, 0.981, 0.982, 0.940$ for $n = 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16$, and the interquartile band widens at $n \in \{12, 14\}$ where the angle-space/restart-budget tension is sharpest. The trend in $r$ is mild, but $p_{\text{opt}}$ collapses geometrically from $\sim 10^{-1}$ at $n=4$ to $\sim 10^{-4}$ at $n=16$, tracking the growth of $\binom{n}{K}$ closely. This is what one would see if the QAOA distribution were near-uniform over the feasible subspace rather than concentrating on the optimum, consistent with \textcite{knapsack2024qaoa} and with \cref{tab:top5}. Note the irregular schedule $K = \mathrm{round}(n/4) = 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4$, so the feasibility-subspace size grows non-monotonically from $4$ to $1820$.

Runtimes complete the picture. Brute force grows from $0.15$~ms to $20$~ms across the range; greedy-Sharpe and rounded Markowitz stay below $0.2$~ms; SA rises from $0.29$~s to $5.3$~s; CBC MIP finishes in $175$~ms at $n=16$, well under its 60-second timeout. QAOA grows from $6$~s at $n=4$ to $\sim 130$~s at $n=16$, dominated by the $O(2^n)$ statevector cost. At $n=16$ the MIP is $\sim 750\times$ faster than QAOA and reaches machine precision with $0\%$ gap; there is no operating point where QAOA wins on either axis, in line with \textcite{shen2025noisy} for classically tractable instances. The methodological value of QAOA here lies in characterising its mechanism, not in runtime. Scaling beyond $n=16$ without the full $O(2^n)$ cost is plausible via Pauli decomposition \parencite{soloviev2025pauli} or circuit cutting \parencite{soloviev2025cutting}, but reaching the regime where the bound of \textcite{tilly2022vqe} becomes tight will require the constraint-preserving mixers tested next, warm-starts, or both \parencite{morapakula2025annealing}.


\subsection{Constraint-preserving XY-ring mixer and Dicke initial state}\label{subsec:xymixer-results}

Every result above uses the transverse-field mixer with a uniform $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$ initial state and a soft penalty. The XY-ring mixer of \cref{eq:HM-XY} commutes with the Hamming-weight operator and, paired with a Dicke initial state $|D^n_K\rangle$, keeps every step of the QAOA dynamics inside the feasible subspace by construction. \Cref{fig:xy-mixer} benchmarks the two protocols at $n=8$, $K=2$, the largest size where each \texttt{expm\_multiply} call stays under a minute while the $\binom{8}{2}=28$ feasible bitstrings inside a $256$-dimensional ambient space make the constraint bite.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{plots/compare/xy_mixer.pdf}
    \caption{XY-ring mixer with a Dicke initial state versus the standard transverse-field mixer with a uniform $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$ start, at $n=8$, $K=2$, $\lambda=2.0$, $A=0.5$ (penalty active for the standard protocol; ignored by the XY-Dicke protocol). Twenty random COBYLA restarts per depth in both cases. Left: scaled approximation ratio versus depth $p \in \{1,2,3,4\}$. Centre: feasibility probability versus depth; the XY-Dicke protocol is at $p_{\text{feas}} = 1$ by construction at every depth, while the standard protocol fluctuates in $[0.47, 0.81]$. Right: wall-clock runtime per solve, log scale; the XY-mixer costs an order of magnitude more per layer because \texttt{expm\_multiply} replaces the factorised single-qubit X rotations.}
    \label{fig:xy-mixer}
\end{figure*}

The XY-Dicke protocol is essentially perfect at every depth: $r_{\text{XY}} = 0.9995, 0.9994, 0.9995, 0.9995$ at $p = 1, 2, 3, 4$, with $E_{\text{XY}} \approx 8.7\times 10^{-3}$ across all four depths, floating-point indistinguishable from the brute-force optimum $-9.71\times 10^{-4}$. Feasibility is exactly unity at every depth, as the commutator test in our pytest suite asserts to machine precision. By contrast the penalty/X-mixer reaches $r_{\text{std}} = 0.977, 0.988, 0.992, 0.980$ with the familiar non-monotonicity at $p=4$ and $p_{\text{feas}} \in [0.47, 0.81]$. The top-1 mass is comparable in both ($p_{\text{opt}} \approx 0.01$--$0.03$) because the Dicke state spreads amplitude uniformly over the $\binom{n}{K}$ feasible bitstrings before the cost layer kicks in: XY-Dicke trades concentration on the single optimum for guaranteed feasibility everywhere else.

The cost shows up in wall-clock: \texttt{expm\_multiply} on a sparse $2^n\times 2^n$ matrix replaces the factorised single-qubit X rotations. At $n=8$ the per-solve runtime is $1.9$~s (standard) versus $21.8$~s (XY-Dicke) at $p=1$, growing to $12.5$~s versus $72.0$~s at $p=4$. The XY-ring Hamiltonian at $n=16$ has $\sim 2\times 10^5$ non-zeros on a $65{,}536\times 65{,}536$ grid, so a single \texttt{expm\_multiply} call at the canonical instance would take an estimated $5$--$10$~minutes per layer, putting a four-depth twenty-restart benchmark in the multi-hour range. That is why we leave the canonical-instance XY-Dicke run to future work and report the $n=8$, $K=2$ benchmark here as a proof of mechanism instead. On hardware the differential closes: the XY-mixer adds two-qubit gates per layer (one per ring edge), but the unitary is implemented by gate decomposition, not Krylov iteration. The overhead reported here is a simulator artefact, not a deployment verdict.

The combined picture from \cref{subsec:warmstart-results,subsec:xymixer-results} is the most useful methodological result of this report: the standard pipeline's two failures (cardinality leakage and stalled $r \approx 0.94$--$0.99$) both have structural fixes flagged in the literature \parencite{zhou2018qaoa,mancilla2026constrained}, and our experiments reproduce both predictions empirically.


\subsection{Summary}\label{subsec:results-summary}

The eight experiments converge on a consistent picture, summarised in \cref{tab:results-summary}: the standard penalty/X-mixer pipeline trails the classical baselines on every axis, warm starts restore monotone depth scaling, and the XY-Dicke variant achieves $r = 0.9995$ with $p_{\text{feas}} = 1$ by construction.

Three findings emerge that the rest of the report defends. First, the standard QAOA pipeline at the chosen scale does converge, but onto the wrong subspace: \cref{tab:top5} shows the four most probable bitstrings are all single-asset selections and the modal outcome is the all-ones state, none of which respect the cardinality constraint. Second, neither $p$ nor $A$ nor $\lambda$ within the standard QAOA template recovers the classical optimum within the fifty-restart budget; the scaled ratio plateaus in the $0.97$--$0.99$ band while the unscaled gap drifts upward by an order of magnitude as $A$ grows. Warm starts and the XY-mixer, the two structural modifications named in the literature, both deliver on their predictions: monotone depth scaling for warm starts, exact feasibility for XY-Dicke. Third, classical methods are strictly better in this regime on every measurable axis: faster by three orders of magnitude, optimal to numerical precision, and deterministic across seeds. The CBC MIP in particular returns the certified optimum in $175$~ms, a useful anchor that this report did not have in earlier drafts. These findings ground the future-work paragraphs in \cref{section:conclusion}, where stacking warm starts on top of the XY-mixer and pushing to larger $n$ via circuit cutting \parencite{soloviev2025cutting} are taken up as the natural next set of design choices.
